AI and democracy: the right to resist optimization

Taiwan’s cyber-ambassador Audrey Tang on why the real danger of AI isn’t that machines imitate humans, but that humans adapt to machines.

EM Elena Marsh · September 12, 2026 · 11 min read

When Audrey Tang talks about artificial intelligence, she rarely starts with the machines. She starts with the room: who is in it, who was left out of it, and what happens to a democracy when the people inside it stop noticing the difference. As Taiwan’s first digital minister and now its cyber-ambassador, Tang has spent a decade building tools that let millions of citizens deliberate together online — and watching, with growing unease, as a handful of optimization systems elsewhere quietly narrowed what public deliberation is allowed to look like.

“We keep asking whether AI can think like a person,” Tang told me over a video call from Taipei. “That is the wrong fear. The real risk is that people start thinking like a recommendation system — reducing every question to whichever answer can be measured, ranked, and served back the fastest.”

It is a subtle distinction, but for Tang it is the whole argument. Optimization, in her telling, is not a neutral tool that democracies can simply adopt. It carries a bias toward whatever is legible to a metric: engagement, throughput, consensus scores. Anything that resists quantification — dissent, ambivalence, the slow work of changing someone’s mind — tends to get optimized away, not because anyone decided it was unimportant, but because no one built a way to count it.

“A healthy democracy needs friction. It needs the right to be illegible to the system for a while. If every disagreement has to resolve into a score before it counts, you haven’t digitized deliberation — you’ve replaced it.”

Building rooms machines can’t flatten

This is why, Tang argues, the platforms Taiwan has built for civic participation — from the vTaiwan consultation process to the Polis-based tools used after the 2014 Sunflower Movement — were designed around a different premise than most AI products: surface disagreement rather than resolve it prematurely, and let a plurality of positions sit next to each other without forcing a single winning answer. The algorithms involved cluster opinions to help people see where common ground already exists, but they do not rank citizens, and they do not optimize for time-on-platform.

“Optimization for what, and optimization for whom, are political questions,” she said. “They should never be answered inside a company’s product roadmap alone.”

The right to resist, not just the right to explain

Much of the current AI-governance conversation, Tang notes, has settled around transparency: explainable models, disclosed training data, audit trails. She supports all of it, but considers it insufficient on its own. Explanation tells a citizen how a system reached its output. It does nothing to guarantee they can opt out of the system’s premises altogether.

What she wants enshrined instead is closer to a civic right: the ability for a community to say that a domain of public life — how a neighborhood is policed, how a curriculum is written, how a public hospital triages care — will not be handed to an optimizer at all, regardless of how well it explains itself. Not every improvement is worth the tradeoff of a democracy quietly rewriting its own values to match what a model can measure.

“We didn’t abolish juries when statistics got good enough to predict verdicts,” Tang said. “We understood that some processes are valuable because of how they involve people, not just because of what they output. AI makes that argument more urgent, not less.”

As more governments look to Taiwan’s model for lessons on digital democracy, Tang’s message resists easy export: no dashboard or protocol will save a public sphere that has already outsourced its judgment. The tools can widen the table. Only people, still willing to disagree in public, can decide what belongs on it.
